Most of the brain's activity is not a response to anything. It is self-generated, it is highly structured,
and it does not stop when a stimulus arrives — the stimulus lands on top of it. Our basic-science work asks
what that ongoing activity is for.

Several strands:

- **Behavioural relevance of transient network interactions.** Spontaneous network states in fMRI and MEG are
  not incidental: their occupancy and transition statistics relate to cognition and to stable individual
  traits.
- **Perception depends on the state you are in.** Which network configuration the brain occupies just before
  a stimulus predicts whether and how that stimulus is perceived — shown for somatosensory detection, and
  studied in visual processing, where we have looked at long-term variability in the neural representation
  against the striking stability of perceptual experience itself.
- **Representational dynamics.** How information about a stimulus is encoded over time, how that interacts
  with the frequency content of the signal, and what happens when perception runs in reverse, as in mental
  imagery.
- **States across wakefulness and sleep.** Whole-brain transitions during the descent into non-REM sleep, and
  what reduced switching between states looks like in insomnia.
- **Shared dynamics between brains.** Audience brain synchrony as a measure of engagement with live
  performance — dance, in collaboration with groups outside neuroimaging.

The connecting question is whether spontaneous dynamics are best understood as noise the brain has to work
around, or as the substrate the brain uses to organise perception in the first place. Our results point at
the second.
